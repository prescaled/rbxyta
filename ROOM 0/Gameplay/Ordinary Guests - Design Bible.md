# Ordinary Guests: Design Bible

## Purpose and status

Detailed design proposal prepared for Rivin and Prescaled on 24 October 2026. This is the consolidated ordinary-guest design for discussion and implementation planning, not a claim that these NPCs are already built.

The Ashbourne must function as a real hotel between its mysteries. Ordinary guests have reasons to visit, habits, relationships, requests and departures. Their small stories are allowed to be funny, awkward, disappointing or warm without becoming horror plots.

**Established direction:** bookings contain 1–4 guests sharing one suitable room; arrivals vary by night; guests can stay across several nights; well-treated guests may extend a stay. This roster is specifically for guests who do not disappear. The three missing cases remain separate story material.

**New proposals:** every named booking below, its appearance, exact routine, dialogue, request choices, numerical weights and production order. The user authorised expanding and recording this design; these additions still need playtesting and final character approval.

The hotel's exact location and present-day year remain unsettled in existing notes. Local events below occur offsite and are proposed, not new established geography. The hotel's dated decor does not require guests to behave as though modern phones do not exist. Paper maps, newspapers and lobby calls are individual preferences or optional interactions.

## The central rule

A guest has a life that continues when nobody is serving them. Most visible behaviour should be ordinary activity, travel between places or social interaction. A guest does not exist solely to stand still until a request is generated.

A useful guest can be recognised by silhouette, one carried object, a habitual action and a way of speaking. They should still be recognisable without a floating name label.

Group size is independent of personality. A quartet can be considerate; a solo traveller can make several requests. No guest type is automatically wealthy, rude, suspicious or generous because of their occupation, accent, age or appearance.

## A booking is not a headcount

A booking holds 1–4 named individuals. Track bookings, occupied rooms, guests present and guests temporarily offsite separately. Four bookings could bring eight people, not necessarily four.

A four-person booking needs a four-person room or approved bed arrangement. A pair may need twin beds rather than a double. Do not place four adults in a room built for two just because the booking system permits four.

Choose the party, suitable room and planned length of stay together. When no suitable room is available, defer that arrival or use a smaller compatible booking; never spawn guests into closed renovation rooms or Room 214 merely to fill capacity.

No room numbers are assigned to this roster yet. Use actual approved room capacity when the playable hotel layout is ready.

## Arrival and booking mix

Proposed starting distribution per new booking: 40% solo, 30% pair, 20% trio, 10% quartet. This produces an average of two guests per booking, but it is a variety target, not a requirement to force one of every size into every shift.

For a small opening test, try 2–4 new bookings when rooms and active population allow. This is a trial alternative to the older ambiguous “3–8 new guests” placeholder, not an additional guaranteed influx. Continuing residents count toward capacity.

Choose an overall night first: quiet, mixed or busy. Then schedule staggered arrivals compatible with that night. A busy night can be many arrivals, several returns from an outing, or overlapping departures; it need not mean everyone asks for help.

Avoid repeating the same named booking on consecutive visits unless they are continuing a stay or deliberately returning later. Do not spawn a second copy of a resident already upstairs.

A guest's arrival window follows their reason for travel. A wedding group arrives before dressing; a contractor returns after work; an early train passenger does not arrive just before their own departure.

## The stay, from arrival to departure

1. Approach reception together or in a loose party. Luggage and appearance match the trip.
2. Queue without blocking the door. A companion may inspect a notice board, adjust luggage or wait in a seat while the lead guest speaks.
3. Confirm room and bed needs. The lead guest is the spokesperson, not the only person recorded as staying.
4. Collect keys, orient themselves and reach the room. Let one guest read signs or walk slightly ahead; avoid identical synchronized movement.
5. Unpack and settle. Show a limited number of appropriate belongings in the room, not a unique set for every bag.
6. Follow a schedule with ordinary choices: rest, lounge, meal if available, offsite event, phone call, conversation.
7. Make a plausible request when a need actually arises. Once addressed, resume their activity.
8. Continue overnight or leave for an ordinary outing. Being away from the hotel is not a disappearance.
9. Decide whether an extension fits their trip and whether a suitable room remains available.
10. Settle the bill, return keys, collect luggage and leave visibly through the normal exit. Close the booking only when all members have a valid departure arrangement.
11. Leave ordinary room traces for cleaning. The room becomes bookable after turnover, not the instant a guest steps out.

## State and continuity

Useful ordinary states: arriving, queued, checking in, settling, in room, in public space, temporarily offsite, sleeping, requesting help, packing, checking out and departed.

“Not visible” is not a state meaning “missing.” A guest upstairs, behind a closed door, asleep or offsite remains part of their booking.

Each guest needs two or three anchor habits and a small choice pool. A preferred chair is an anchor; using another chair because it is occupied is a natural variation. Do not randomly rewrite the personality every night.

Schedules should use broad windows rather than every guest moving at the same exact second. Never interrupt a coherent conversation solely to make an NPC hit a timestamp.

When a conversation or task is interrupted, resume at a sensible point. A guest who already received towels should not demand the same towels after returning from dinner.

## Distinct people within groups

Maintain the relationship and each member's current activity. One may collect the key while another watches bags; one returns from dinner early; one goes down for water while the rest sleep.

Use short reactions: a companion answers an unnecessary question, corrects the departure time, lends a pen, saves a seat or nudges someone to lower their voice.

Groups meet up for events that matter, but need not move as a permanent formation. Show a brief regrouping pause at the lobby exit instead of teleporting separated members into line.

Members can disagree without becoming hostile. Resolve ordinary friction through compromise: a quieter lounge seat, a cardigan instead of changing everyone's room temperature, or one member leaving early for their appointment.

Keep the party roster stable during the stay. A new named companion is a deliberate booking change, not a random substitute.

## Requests and satisfaction

Requests come from a cause: rain, an event, missing equipment, comfort, a room fault or a departure plan. Most have one clear completion condition.

Proposed starting pacing: typically 0–2 service requests per booking per night, with occasional authored sequences. A quartet may ask for four towels in one request; it does not require four separate interruptions.

Allow one active service request per booking in the first version. Keep a small hotel-wide active cap, initially three, and queue other low-urgency needs. Tune these values against actual shift length.

Separate inconvenience from consequence. A short delay on an extra pillow is not equivalent to a wake-up call after the agreed departure time. Only use deadlines that can actually be met within the schedule.

Satisfaction may start from a neutral-comfortable baseline. Improve it through resolved needs and reliability; lower it through genuine failures. Do not repeatedly subtract points every second or punish a guest's unrelated disappointment.

Carry a small memory of relevant outcomes through the same stay: “the lamp was fixed” or “we missed our requested call.” Longer-term memory between visits is optional later work.

A quiet, content guest may make no requests and still count as a successful stay. Gifts, tips and praise are not guaranteed.

## Stays and extensions

Proposed normal stay: 1–3 nights, with a four-night cap for an initial test. A happy guest may request an extra night if their itinerary permits and capacity exists.

An extension is not automatic just because satisfaction is high. A wedding has ended; a booked train still leaves; a contractor may need employer approval. Offer trip-specific reasons: another survey day, a postponed match or one more day of walking.

An unhappy ordinary guest can leave early through a visible normal checkout. Give the actual reason briefly, close the booking and keep the event separate from the missing-case system.

## Normality alongside horror

These 20 bookings are ordinary guests. None secretly becomes a predator, disappears, resets history or reveals that a companion never existed.

They can notice a noisy pipe, a power interruption or an unsettling corridor and react plausibly. Some ask for reassurance; some stay in their room; others continue reading. Their reaction does not have to explain the hotel's mystery.

Do not make every unusual habit an anomaly clue. Someone practising an interview alone is simply nervous. A camera bag does not automatically contain paranormal evidence.

Their reliable departures matter: all named members are accounted for, luggage leaves and the bill closes normally. This provides a trustworthy contrast to the three missing cases.

## First build recommendation

Start with six bookings: Martin Pike (solo), Leena Shah (solo), Gwen and Arthur Ellis (pair), Aisha and Tom Reed (pair), the wedding trio (Nia, Beth and Callum), and the road crew quartet (Dawn, Pete, Ivo and Kelly).

Together these cover 13 unique people, all four booking sizes, early and late schedules, public and private routines, practical and social requests, departures and optional extensions. They are a development selection, not a requirement to have all 13 present at once.

The other fourteen bookings are a content library for later variety. Build shared behaviours once, then give them character-specific details. No modelling or Studio changes are part of this notes update.
