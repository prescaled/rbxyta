# Ordinary Guests: Activities and Encounters

Design library, 24 October 2026. These are selectable ordinary behaviours for the proposed roster, not a checklist that every guest must perform. Any service or destination depends on the actual approved hotel facilities.

## Public-space activity library

Give each booking two or three signature activities and a few compatible alternatives. Public spaces need both movement and stillness.

### Reception and arrival

- Search the correct pocket for a reservation note, find it and continue without restarting the conversation.
- Move a bag out of the next guest's way.
- Let a companion speak after initially answering for them.
- Read a notice while waiting, then return when called.
- Quietly confirm whether beds are twin, double or a larger agreed arrangement.
- Ask where wet coats may go before laying one on furniture.
- Check the departure time with a companion rather than asking reception to settle a personal disagreement.
- Mishear a room number once, read the key sleeve and correct it. This is a mundane mistake, not a changing-room incident.

### Lounge and waiting

- Turn a page, fold a newspaper to make space or put a bookmark in before speaking.
- Sit near a lamp, window or table according to the activity.
- Move to another available chair if the preferred seat is taken.
- Write a postcard, cross out a sentence and start again.
- Study a route on a phone or map; the object suits the character.
- Doze briefly, wake naturally and collect belongings.
- Charge a device at an approved socket without stretching a lead across a walkway.
- Watch the weather and decide to postpone an outing.
- Practise a presentation quietly, stop when another guest sits nearby and resume later.
- Wait for a taxi with bags ready; look towards the door without pacing forever.
- Work through a crossword with a willing companion.
- Take a permitted photograph that does not intrude on another guest.

### Meals and refreshments, where provided

- Carry one drink carefully, set it down and continue the original activity.
- Compare menus before ordering rather than generating separate demands for every item.
- Pass a companion the food they usually prefer.
- Ask for a missing cup or utensil once.
- Read while waiting for service, then put the book away.
- Finish a meal, collect belongings and leave the table available.
- Return a borrowed tray to an agreed spot if that suits the character.
- Choose an offsite meal if the hotel's service has closed; do not fabricate an open kitchen.

### Room and corridor

- Set a bag down, hang a coat and check the bedside lamp.
- Place shoes together and change out of outdoor clothing.
- Knock once, identify themselves to their companion and wait for the door.
- Lower their voice when passing another room.
- Check a key before entering the correct room.
- Leave a do-not-disturb indication and sleep.
- Pack before bed to prepare for an early departure.
- Return to collect a forgotten ordinary item, then visibly complete the original journey.
- Open the room door slightly for a delivery, acknowledge it and close it.
- Pause for someone with luggage to pass in a narrow corridor.

## A request needs a cause and an end

For each request, establish the trigger, who speaks, what counts as completion, the guest's next action and the fallback if service is unavailable.

A fulfilled request should change something observable. A new lamp is used. Wet items are placed in the drying space. The group leaves once everyone is ready. Avoid an NPC thanking someone while remaining stuck in the requesting animation.

### Comfort and room requests

1. **Extra pillow:** guest prefers a higher pillow; deliver one if stocked; they place it on their bed and settle. A calm refusal when none remain is better than an impossible task.
2. **Extra blanket:** one member feels cold; supply a blanket before changing the whole group's heating preference.
3. **Additional towels:** rain or an insufficient room allocation triggers one request for the appropriate quantity. Supply and mark the booking's request complete.
4. **Reading light:** the guest cannot use a chosen reading place; fix a genuine fault or offer a usable alternative seat.
5. **Rattling fixture:** an audible, actual noise disturbs rest; repair it or offer an available suitable room. Do not repeat the complaint once resolved.
6. **Stuck curtain:** guest wants privacy or sleep; restore normal use and let them finish settling.
7. **Heating concern:** investigate the actual room state; offer a reasonable alternative if no repair is immediately possible.
8. **Missing hangers:** guest has event clothing; supply a limited appropriate number and show the clothes being put away.
9. **Correct bed setup:** identify the mismatch before settling where possible; resolve using an approved room or arrangement. Never pretend a double satisfies a confirmed twin request.
10. **Quiet-room preference:** honour when capacity permits; otherwise explain the available options. It is a preference, not a license to displace another guest.

### Trip and departure requests

11. **Wake-up call:** agree a time within the playable schedule; deliver once; guest acknowledges and begins preparing. Missing it changes their plans only as plausibly authored.
12. **Taxi confirmation:** use the game's available transport abstraction; confirm the arrangement and have the guest wait with bags. This does not require real external booking.
13. **Local directions:** offer only authored, consistent directions to an offsite destination. Do not invent map routes or real-world advice.
14. **Luggage holding:** agree a storage location and collection time; retain the bags until the identified booking collects them.
15. **Late checkout:** approve only when turnover and the next booking permit it; communicate the agreed limit.
16. **One-night extension:** check itinerary and room capacity, update the expected departure and continue the same booking.
17. **Split departure:** one member leaves early; update that individual's status while the rest remain. The last person settles the remaining agreed checkout.
18. **Receipt correction:** fix an ordinary spelling or payer detail once. This routine must not resemble the Shaw case's impossible completed bill.

### Preparation and equipment requests

19. **Iron:** lend only available equipment, provide an agreed return point and finish the request when supplied. Track the loan separately until returned.
20. **Sewing kit:** a loose button or hem before an event; provide the stocked kit; the guest repairs their own clothing.
21. **Spare pen:** a small finite favour; the guest completes the form or puzzle.
22. **Drying space:** return from rain; use a suitable available space and have guests retrieve their things before departure.
23. **Wrapping material:** protect a fragile personal object if material is stocked; do not turn the hotel into a full packing service.
24. **Extra chair:** only if safe space and stock exist; otherwise offer an appropriate public table.
25. **Loan charger:** later optional service, dependent on era, approved equipment and compatibility. Record the loan rather than producing unlimited copies.
26. **Help finding an item:** guest retraces a known ordinary route; object is found at a plausible location. Return it or record it as ordinary lost property.
27. **Vase or suitable jug:** flowers arrive with a leisure booking; agree a substitute when necessary.
28. **Table use:** a group asks to play a game or sort papers; offer a vacant suitable table and a normal ending time.
29. **Meal timing question:** give the actual service window; late guests may choose an alternative.
30. **Noise complaint:** identify the source, request a reasonable volume change and allow the complaining guest to settle once it is quieter.

## Suggested priorities and request endings

Immediate acknowledgement is useful, but most needs can wait briefly. Comfort requests have generous windows; event preparation and transport have meaningful scheduled deadlines. Maintenance concerns pause use of the affected object where necessary.

If a requested service does not exist, the guest chooses a plausible fallback: use their own alarm, read elsewhere, decline an extension or arrange another meal. A limitation is not automatically a service failure.

Give each request a terminal outcome: fulfilled, alternative accepted, politely declined, cancelled by the guest or missed. Do not leave abandoned tasks active after checkout.

A guest may decide they no longer need an iron. Cancel the request cleanly and free any reserved equipment. Do not punish completion after a cancellation that was never communicated.

## Guest-to-guest scenes

These are brief optional encounters between compatible guests, not mandatory interactions or a reason to change their trips.

- **Martin and Ruth:** Ruth hears his rehearsal and suggests a shorter opening sentence. He tries it once.
- **Leena and Saira:** compare weather observations; Mel asks which route has the least hill.
- **Denise and Gwen:** Gwen admires a mug; Denise manages to say the price without apologising. A purchase can remain dialogue rather than a new economy system.
- **Omar and Callum:** Callum notices a twisted tie and asks before helping.
- **Felix and the road crew:** one nod of recognition over early departures, then everyone allows the others to rest.
- **Joyce and Leena:** Joyce asks about a bird; Leena happily answers a single ordinary question.
- **Ruth and Val:** share a table without needing to talk continuously.
- **Colin and June:** disagree politely about an object's age; Bea and Paula exchange a patient look.
- **Beth and Tess:** Beth asks to borrow the lobby mirror for a moment; Tess moves her book aside.
- **Kit and Len:** recognise each other's neatly organised cases. Neither starts repairing hotel wiring.
- **Tom and Marcus:** compare travel snacks while their families finish arrangements.
- **Arthur and Ben:** Arthur offers unsolicited game advice, is immediately wrong and laughs.
- **Patrick and Felix:** quietly negotiate the last empty lounge chair; one is leaving shortly.
- **Nia and Farah:** both have schedules; they compare how many people actually follow them.
- **Mel and Denise:** Mel is the first person to say they liked something without asking what it cost.
- **Audible group boundary:** a lively group notices someone sleeping and moves its conversation rather than needing a confrontation every time.

Scenes should not fire when a participant is asleep, leaving, busy in another scene or absent. Reserve the required seats and objects first; cancel gracefully if the conditions change.

## Ordinary misunderstandings with visible resolution

- Similar umbrellas are briefly confused. Both owners inspect them and exchange them back.
- A key sleeve is in a coat pocket instead of a bag. The guest finds it while reception is still checking.
- A squeak is a suitcase wheel, not a sound from a locked room.
- A lounge chair appears empty because the owner stepped away. They return and negotiate another seat.
- Two people thought the other packed the shoes. The shoes are visibly inside the garment bag.
- An early caller has the wrong time written down. Correct it against the agreed booking note.
- A coat on a chair looks like a person at a glance; the owner collects it in an ordinary scene.
- A guest talking alone is rehearsing or using a phone; their next action makes that clear.

Use these sparingly. The aim is believable everyday interpretation, not repeatedly tricking the audience into expecting a scare.

## Returns, thanks and room traces

A returning guest may remember a preferred room, a solved lamp problem or a previous conversation. Keep the memory specific and modest. Do not have every stranger behave like an intimate friend after one towel delivery.

Possible endings: a spoken thanks, a nod, a brief note, permission to keep a harmless sketch, a postcard on a later day or no ceremony at all. Gratitude should fit the character.

Room traces communicate use: a moved chair, a folded newspaper, a damp towel, used cups, crumbs, wrapping paper or a forgotten inexpensive item. Keep valuable personal objects with their owners unless an authored lost-property event explains otherwise.

Ordinary lost property is eventually collected, forwarded through an abstract hotel process or logged unclaimed. It does not mean its owner vanished. Avoid leaving every guest's luggage behind; that visual belongs to the missing cases.
